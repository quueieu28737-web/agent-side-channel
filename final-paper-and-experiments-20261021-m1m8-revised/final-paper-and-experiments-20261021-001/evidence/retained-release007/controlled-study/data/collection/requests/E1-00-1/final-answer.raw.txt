{"text":"Tidal power is electricity made by capturing the movement of seawater as tides rise and fall, driven mainly by the Moon’s gravity and, to a lesser extent, the Sun’s. One approach uses underwater turbines, much like wind turbines, placed in channels where tidal currents flow strongly; moving water turns their blades and spins generators. Another approach builds a barrage or lagoon that holds back water, then releases it through turbines when the water levels on either side differ. Because tides follow predictable cycles, operators can forecast when power will be available more reliably than they can forecast wind or sunshine, although generation is intermittent rather than constant. Tidal projects can provide low-carbon electricity over long periods, but construction can be expensive and suitable sites are limited to places with strong currents or large tidal ranges. Equipment must withstand saltwater, storms, and marine growth, and projects may alter habitats, sediment movement, or the passage of fish and boats. Careful siting and monitoring can reduce some impacts, but they cannot remove every trade-off. Tidal power is therefore a promising regional resource, rather than a universal replacement for other electricity sources."}
