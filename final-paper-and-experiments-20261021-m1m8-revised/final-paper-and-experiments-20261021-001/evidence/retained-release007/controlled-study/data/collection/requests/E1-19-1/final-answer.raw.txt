{"text":"Weather forecasting begins with observations collected from weather stations, ships, aircraft, balloons, and satellites. These instruments measure conditions such as temperature, air pressure, wind, humidity, and rainfall, giving forecasters a picture of the atmosphere at a particular time. Powerful computer models then use physical laws and these observations to estimate how air and moisture will move and change over the coming hours and days. Because the atmosphere is complex and small measurement errors can grow, forecasts become less certain farther into the future, so a detailed prediction for tomorrow is usually more reliable than one for next week. Forecasters compare model results with local geography and recent weather, using experience to judge whether a predicted storm, heat wave, or change in wind is plausible. A forecast is therefore not a promise but an informed estimate, and checking updates can help people make practical decisions about clothing, travel, outdoor work, and safety."}
