{"text":"Coastal dunes are hills or ridges of sand built along shorelines, where wind, waves, and plants continually reshape the landscape. Most begin when wind carries dry beach sand inland and drops it around obstacles such as driftwood, stones, or low vegetation. As grasses and other hardy plants take root, their stems slow the wind and trap more grains, gradually helping dunes grow and hold their shape. Dunes are not fixed barriers: storms can cut into them, move sand offshore, or push it farther inland, while calmer conditions allow rebuilding. These sandy formations provide habitat for specialized plants and animals, and their changing slopes create varied conditions for life. They also help protect inland areas by absorbing wave energy and storing sand that may replenish beaches after erosion. Because trampling, construction, and rising seas can weaken dune systems, many coastal communities protect vegetation, limit access in sensitive places, and restore dunes with native plants. Their natural movement is part of how a shoreline responds to changing weather and sea conditions."}
