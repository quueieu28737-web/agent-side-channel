\section{Controlled Transfer Tests}\label{sec:controlled-placement}\label{sec:tau-cover-bridge}
The audit labels many reuse claims \emph{unresolved}. A useful audit should flag the transfers that then fail and should not flag every transfer indiscriminately. We therefore wrote down seven transfer claims, the checks that bear on each, a predicted outcome and numeric decision thresholds, and committed them to the artifact repository before running experiments E5--E7 (Table~\ref{tab:transfer-predictions}). A defense counts as \emph{protected} when the best held-out attacker's balanced accuracy (BA) has a 95\% upper bound below 0.60 and AUROC below 0.65, and as \emph{not protected} when the lower bound exceeds 0.60. The best attacker is the maximum over all evaluated attackers, including the clean-trained $D_0$, which favours the attacker. The same authors wrote the checks and the predictions, so this is a falsifiable internal test, not external validation.

All experiments run locally on saved outputs, the pinned $\tau$-bench retail simulator and loopback sockets. A byte-transparent relay records every TLS record; only post-handshake application records are counted, and TLS~1.3 session tickets are disabled. No model API, production tool or real account is used. E5--E7 use no LLM planner, so they isolate boundaries rather than evaluate an autonomous agent.

\subsection{Transport: buffered and streaming emitters}
\paragraph{Buffered emitter}
The corpus has one prompted brief and one prompted expanded \texttt{gpt-6-luna} response for each of 24 topics (12 training and 12 test topics); these are designed conditions, not natural length samples. In a two-link TLS~1.2 replay, a frontend that decodes and re-chunks the upstream text leaves the terminal link 100\% separable when only the upstream link is padded; padding both links reduces the evaluated classifier to 50\%. This reproduces NetEcho's mechanism \cite{netecho2025}. With workload-matched denominators, 512-byte buckets cost 2.30$\times$ and 1.43$\times$ for two sentence-prefix workloads (T2, T3); a cap fitted on training lengths (1,280 bytes) yields 50\% at 2.18$\times$ for verbosity (T1). Under offsets, 1024-byte buckets and random tails, refitting ($D_2$) gains 13.5--50.0 points over $D_0$; constant caps give no gain. Supplement S11 retains all cells.

\paragraph{E5: the same texts as a token stream}
These figures describe an emitter that frames a response only after generation is complete. LLM APIs usually stream, and the streaming attacks in our corpus observe per-token records \cite{weiss2024prompt,whisperleak2025}. E5 emits the same 48 texts as server-sent chunk events, one TLS record per token, over TLS~1.2 (AES-128-GCM) and TLS~1.3 (AES-256-GCM). Token boundaries use o200k\_base as a proxy; the generating model's tokenizer and timing were not saved, so latency is reported in token intervals. The framings are per-event random padding of 1--64 bytes (an obfuscation field), a constant 144-byte event, 8-token batches padded to a constant size, and constant events plus dummy events up to the largest training token count. Besides $D_0$ and $D_2$ threshold and sequence classifiers, a closed-world attacker profiles all 48 candidate responses in an independent run and matches each observation to the nearest record-length sequence.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer-streaming} resolves P1--P3. Per-event padding hides token lengths but not their number. Verbosity remains 100\% separable for both attackers, and identification falls only from 100\% to 58--62\% (chance 2.1\%). Batching sends fewer envelopes (0.21$\times$ bytes) and drives $D_0$ to 50\%, while $D_2$ still scores 100\%. A constant-shape stream reaches 50\% at 2.42$\times$ stream bytes and doubles completion time (208 versus 105 token intervals); two test outputs overflow the fitted count and remain identifiable. The buffered cap's 2.19$\times$ is a different cost object: its first byte arrives only after the full response. The 2.18$\times$ result therefore does not price a streaming deployment; Check~1 and R9 flagged exactly this transfer.

\subsection{Execution: state scope and randomized cover}
\paragraph{State scope}
We replay the 115 public retail reference programs. Same-item writes change workflow state: the item-modification tool sets the status to \texttt{pending (item modified)} and appends a refund entry when item and price are unchanged (173/291 pre-insertion units). A no-change requirement $f(s,a)=s$ thus differs from repeated-call idempotence $f(f(s,a),a)=f(s,a)$. Cancelling another simulated user's order preserves the active account in every unit while changing the shared database in every unit. One cover call at a known first or last position is removed by a position or count rule with 100\% accuracy. These are properties of one simulator and of a deliberately weak cover design (Supplement S11).

\paragraph{E6: randomized cover against policy-aware attackers}
E6 asks what a properly randomized cover achieves in the same tool-dispatch view, which shows ordered tool names and response lengths, including failures. Cover calls are drawn from other users' reference programs, inserted at uniformly random positions, and executed in a per-session sandbox (shadow) or, as a control, live. The policies add one cancellation with probability $0.5$ or $1$, pad the cancellation count to two, interleave one or two complete decoy programs, or equalize the program \emph{shape}. The last pads every tool's count to its maximum over the public program population, a label-free template. All parameters were fixed before execution. Attackers are gradient boosting and logistic regression over tool counts, bigrams and response lengths, fitted on clean ($D_0$) or defended ($D_2$) sessions, plus a count rule. We use task-grouped 5-fold cross-validation over all 115 tasks and three seeds, with two targets: cancellation (18 tasks) and return (32 tasks).

The dispatch columns of Table~\ref{tab:transfer-cover} show that adding one cancellation always takes $D_0$ to 50\% BA, but $D_2$ recovers 100\% (P2). Equalizing the cancellation count defeats the count rule (50\%), whereas a learned model using correlated tools reaches 76.9\% [64.8, 88.0] (P4). Decoy programs, the read-only defense pattern of WRA \cite{jeong2026wra}, leave 83.5--91.9\% because most decoy programs contain no cancellation. In this view, only shape equalization reaches chance (50.0\% and 54.5\%), at 6.7 cover calls per real call. Sandboxed cover keeps database equality at 100\%; the same cancellation executed live yields 0\%.

\subsection{E7: composing execution and transport on the agent--tool link}
Figure~\ref{fig:system-model} draws observation and execution as separate paths; a remote tool server joins them on one encrypted connection. E7 replays four cover policies over a TLS~1.2 agent--tool link with one length-prefixed request and response per call (4,140 sessions). Framing is raw, 1024-byte buckets or constant (512-byte requests; 3,072-byte responses, the 95th percentile, with larger responses padded to 1024-byte multiples). The network observer sees direction-tagged record lengths and no tool names. In addition to the learned models, a profiling attacker learns a size-to-tool table by calling the public tool API on training-fold calls, decodes test traces and applies the dispatch attacks.

The network columns of Table~\ref{tab:transfer-cover} report the outcomes. Under raw framing, request size alone names the tool for 95.7\% of calls, compared with a 29.4\% majority baseline. Decoding therefore carries the dispatch-view weaknesses onto the encrypted link, giving 72.7--95.7\% BA for count and decoy cover (P5). Framing without cover removes tool identity, but message counts and response overflows remain. Bucketed return inference keeps 73.9\% [65.0, 82.6], and the other framing-only cells are inconclusive at 53.6--60.1\% (P6, partly confirmed). Shape equalization without framing leaks through cover response sizes (return 60.5\% [54.7, 66.4]). Only both together reach 50.0\% for return and 54.4\% [47.5, 61.7] for cancellation (AUROC 0.50), with 100\% database equality. This combination costs 26.3$\times$ bytes and 7.7$\times$ round trips, and the cancellation upper bound misses the pre-registered threshold by 1.7 points (P7, partly confirmed).

\paragraph{Online dispatchers}
E6 and E7 let each policy see the whole program. A dispatcher serving an autonomous agent sees only past calls. Our agent pipeline (Supplement S13) records tool-calling trajectories, applies online versions of the policies and replays them over TLS. No LLM was available for this revision, so the pipeline was run with two stand-in planners. With the scripted planner, online shape equalization leaves cancellation at 63.8\% [57, 71] in the dispatch view and 57.5\% [50, 65] with constant framing, because unmet counts must be flushed at the end of the session. A planner that inserts extra reads overflows the template in 20.9\% of sessions. The near-chance result of P7 therefore depends on plan knowledge, and LLM planners remain untested.

\subsection{What the predictions show}
Five claims are confirmed and two are partly confirmed; none is refuted. Every transfer that the checks marked unresolved and that we could test either failed or left evidence too weak to license reuse at our sample sizes. Three findings are not visible in the buffered and fixed-position experiments. A cost figure belongs to its emitter: a buffered cap does not price a stream. A clean-trained attacker can understate leakage under batching and probabilistic cover (P2). Cover and framing protect different projections, so each leaves the other's channel open, and their composition is the first configuration near chance. The P6 and P7 intervals reflect 18 cancellation and 32 return tasks, and the test does not establish how the checks perform on deployments chosen by others.

\soktakeaway{SoK Takeaway 3 (Bind the projection the emitter produces)}{A padding result applies to the record sequence it equalized. Buffered caps, per-event padding and batching protect different projections and have different cost objects (bytes, completion time, first-byte delay). A low clean-trained accuracy is not a protection claim.}

\soktakeaway{SoK Takeaway 4 (State, inference and transport are separate obligations)}{Same-value tool inputs can change state, and account equality can coexist with global database changes. Randomized single-verb cover and decoy programs leave learned or count attacks far above chance. In our tests only shape-level equalization combined with constant framing approached chance, at 26$\times$ bytes and 7.7$\times$ calls.}
